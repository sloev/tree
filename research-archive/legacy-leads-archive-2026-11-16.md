I. SCOPE & METHODOLOGICAL NOTE ON NON-TRADITIONAL & ILLICIT LINEAGES
---
Genealogical research in Danish parish registers (Kirkebøger), census records 
(Folketællinger), probate records (Skifteprotokoller), and military/adels archives 
frequently reveals complex family dynamics that extend beyond formal nuclear 
marriages. This dossier explicitly incorporates:
1. Legitimate Bloodlines: Standard marital births and generational descents.
2. Illegitimate & Extramarital Bloodlines ("Uægte Børn"): Births recorded out of 
   wedlock where biological paternity is established via church baptismal records, 
   paternity cases (Almendings- og Sognefogedprotokoller), or court documents.
3. Acknowledged Paternity & Social Fatherhood: Instances of step-parenting, 
   fosterage (Plejebørn), and recognized illegitimate children who carry biological 
   bloodlines.
4. Adoptive & Feudal Alliance Ties: Legal and social adoptions, land transfers, 
   and dynastic marriages across Danish, Pomeranian, and German nobility.

# Potential genealogical leads — research register

## How to use this register

This file deliberately preserves potential, unverified, contradictory and partially sourced leads. A lead is not a fact merely because it appears here. Preserve the exact wording and provenance, assign a status, and revisit it when new evidence becomes available. Do not delete weak leads simply because they are unresolved; annotate negative searches and explain what was searched.

Suggested statuses:
- **Documented clue** — a source explicitly names the person, event or relationship, but the original underlying record may not yet have been inspected.
- **Strong lead** — a credible source or index points to a specific record or relationship.
- **Hypothesis** — plausible but not yet directly supported.
- **Conflict** — sources disagree or identity/relationship is ambiguous.
- **Open search** — a defined archival or online search remains to be done.

## A. von Kleist — Muttrin and Damen branches

### A1. Dubislaf / Dubislaff “Bratta” on Damen

**Status:** Documented clue from the 2025 family-association genealogy; underlying medieval enfeoffment should still be inspected.

The 2025 *Geschichte des Geschlechts v. Kleist*, Muttrin-Damensche Linie, Damen volume, says:
- Dubislaf, also called Bratta, on Damen, is dated 1477 and died circa 1482.
- He was the son of Henning (I.M.8) and Margarethe von Wedelstädt of Gülzow.
- A collective feudal letter dated 13 April 1477 mentions him and five sons: Drews, Curt, Bisprow, Peter and Pribislaf.
- It says Dubislaf held Damen and half of Bublitz; his brother Bisprow held the other half.
- It describes two marriages: first to Catharina von Podewils, mother of Drews, Curt and Bisprow; second to an unnamed von Glasenapp of Gramenz, mother of Peter and Pribislaf.

Source: https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Muttrin/Damen.pdf, especially printed pages 2–3 / PDF text around lines 89–136.

**Important:** This is a family genealogy compiled from older family history and documentary references. It is a strong roadmap, not a substitute for checking the cited 1477 feudal letter (reference 125), 1486 enfeoffment (reference 192), and the source notes.

### A2. Prissebur of Muttrin and the 1364 document

**Status:** Documented clue; the family association's 2025 Muttrin volume explicitly cites a 1364 document.

The 2025 *Muttrin* volume says the second son of Klest (M.1) appears in document 83 of 1364 as “Prißebur, der Klest heißt von Muttrin”. It says the names Jarßlaff and Henning as Prissebur's sons come from a family genealogy of 1576 and an older letter concerning Glineke. It then identifies a Henning of Muttrin in the 1407 material.

Source: https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Muttrin/Muttrin.pdf, introduction, PDF text around lines 223–260.

The older L. Quandt genealogy (published in the family history) discusses the early descent and its reasoning, including the difficulty of dating the alleged earlier family letter and the 1364 Prissebur reference:
https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Genealogie/genealogie.pdf.

### A3. Keep the two claims distinct

The 2025 *Damen* volume says Jarislav brought the Damen estate into the family around 1365. That is not the same claim as Prissebur appearing in a 1364 document as a Muttrin landholder. The family history treats Muttrin and Damen as branches/estates within the wider Muttrin-Damensche line.

Sources:
- Damen volume: https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Muttrin/Damen.pdf
- Muttrin volume: https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Muttrin/Muttrin.pdf

Do not state that Prissebur personally acquired Damen in 1364. Do not conflate the Muttrin line's early generations with the later Damen branch without tracing the numbered family-tree references.

### A4. Remaining medieval uncertainty

The older Quandt reconstruction itself contains argued identifications and inferred generations. The 2025 volumes explain that the early generations rely on contemporary documents together with later family records and a “probable arrangement” of persons. The medieval pedigree should therefore be represented with source-specific confidence, not as uniformly proven descent.

Potential next checks:
1. Obtain document 83 (1364) and inspect the original name, property and witness context.
2. Obtain document 125 (13 April 1477) and document 192 (28 December 1486) cited in the Damen volume.
3. Compare the 1576 family genealogy with the later 19th-century family history and the 2025 revision.
4. Keep any “Prisse Kleistes genannt” woman in the 1577 settlement separate from Prissebur of Muttrin unless a source proves a connection.

## B. Gaardsted / Rolsøgård

### B1. Karen Christensdatter and Claus Gaardsted

**Status:** Strong lead for a sibling relationship; parentage of Claus and Karen remains unresolved.

The Rolsøgård G302 alphabetical probate index, under Niels Jensen Degn in Vrinners, probate no. 11, 14 May 1787, folio 39, includes this note:

“Arv i boet til afdødes stedbørn efter Christen Christensen Tohde? foged i Dejret (skifte 1764) til datter Karen Christensdatter LV: broder Claus Gaardsted forpagter på Vosnæsgård.”

This supports that Karen Christensdatter was identified as sister of Claus Gaardsted, tenant farmer at Vosnæsgård. It points to an earlier 1764 probate for Christen Christensen Tohde, bailiff in Dejret.

Source: https://slaegt.dk/media/jnnnvuml/alfabetisk-navneliste-og-skifteuddrag-til-rols%C3%B8g%C3%A5rd-gods-g302-mols-herred-1778-1816-1818.pdf.

**Caution:** The index wording says “afdødes stedbørn efter Christen Christensen Tohde” (the deceased's stepchildren after Christen Christensen Tohde). It does not establish that Tohde was Karen's biological father; it may indicate step-parentage. The 1787 probate is Niels Jensen Degn's probate, not Karen's own probate. The index is a pointer to a 1764 case, not the original case itself.

### B2. The 1764 Tohde probate

**Status:** Open archival search.

Locate the earlier Rolsøgård estate probate series for 1764 (before the indexed 1778–1816 volume). Search under Christen Christensen Tohde, possible spelling variants, and Dejret. Establish:

## 8. Genealogical scope: all well-founded family connections

**Research scope is not limited to legitimate descent or conventional pedigree lines.** The objective is to document real, well-founded connections among people and families, including connections that historical records may have marginalised, obscured or described inconsistently.

Include, where supported:
- Biological parentage and descent, whether the child was born within or outside marriage.
- Acknowledged, recorded or otherwise well-supported illegitimate children and non-marital paternity/maternity.
- Marriage, remarriage, partnerships and other documented unions, including relationships that overlap in time.
- Adoption and legally or socially recognised parent-child relationships.
- Step-parent, foster, guardian and household relationships where they are historically meaningful.
- Other documented connections (for example, a recognised fatherhood, maintenance, probate, inheritance or guardianship relationship) that help explain the family network.

### Relationship labels and evidence

Never collapse different kinds of connection into a single generic parent/spouse link. Distinguish at least:
- **Biological** — asserted or supported biological parentage.
- **Marital/partner** — marriage or documented partnership; does not itself prove biological parentage.
- **Adoptive** — adoption or legally recognised adoptive parentage.
- **Step** — relationship through a parent's marriage/partnership, without implying biological descent.
- **Foster/guardian/household** — care or household connection, not biological descent unless separately evidenced.
- **Acknowledged/social/legal parent** — a person recognised as a parent in a source, with biological status separately stated if unknown or disputed.

For every proposed relationship, record what the source actually establishes. A baptism entry naming a father, a probate statement, a court case, a family chronicle, a marriage record and a later family tree do not all prove the same thing. Note whether a source establishes biological parentage, legal recognition, social parenthood, marriage, or merely a claim.

Use calibrated confidence and preserve disagreements:
- **Established**: direct, credible evidence for the specific relationship.
- **Probable**: strong converging evidence, but not conclusive.
- **Possible/claimed**: a plausible lead or a claim that needs corroboration.
- **Disputed/rejected**: conflicting evidence or a link shown to be erroneous.

Do not treat illegitimacy as a reason to exclude a person or connection. Do not infer illegitimacy from silence, stigma, a gap in records, or a later surname. Equally, do not turn a historical allegation or rumour about fatherhood into a fact without evidence. Use sensitive and respectful wording, quote historical terminology only when necessary to explain a source, and distinguish a contemporary allegation from a proven biological relationship.

### Data-model implications

Before adding complex or overlapping relationships, inspect the data model and rendering. The current `person()` helper in `data/build_data.py` has a single `spouse` parameter; this may be insufficient for multiple marriages or concurrent partnerships. Do not overwrite one union with another or rely on one-sided spouse fields. Extend the model deliberately if needed, with relationship type, dates, source and confidence, while keeping biological parentage separate from union records. Ensure GEDCOM/export and visualisations preserve these distinctions.

**Research principle:** completeness means investigating all credible lines and connections, not lowering the evidentiary standard. A well-documented illegitimate line belongs in the history just as much as a legitimate one.

## Von Kleist / Muttrin — additional source comparison

A second, more important source pass checked the 2025 Muttrin volume against the family association's 2021/2022 *Urkundenbuch* edition.

### 1364: Prissebur of Muttrin is a real documentary reference

The 2025 Muttrin volume, printed pages 5–6, explicitly says that the second son of Klest (M.1) is mentioned in Urkunde 83 of 1364 as a co-owner of land at Glineke: “Prißebur, der Klest heißt von Muttrin.” It then says the names Jarßlaff and Henning as Prissebur's sons come from an early family genealogy of 1576 and an older letter in which Prissebur acted for himself and his two sons concerning Glineke.

This is not the same as the acquisition of Damen around 1365. The earlier correction in Segment 1 of the extension that said there was no 1364 reference was wrong as a blanket statement. The 1364 reference is present in the Muttrin volume; the c.1365 date concerns Damen.

The 2025 authors expressly qualify the early pedigree: Quandt's arrangement uses individual contemporary documents together with family records made considerably later, and is a *wahrscheinliche Anordnung* (probable arrangement). Accordingly:
- Prissebur's appearance in 1364 is a documented clue reported by the modern family-history edition, with the original Urkunde 83 still to be inspected.
- The parent link from Klest to Prissebur and the sons Jarßlaff/Henning have a different evidentiary basis (later family genealogy / older letter as reported by the edition).
- Later generations should be checked against each cited document rather than assigned the same confidence as the 1364 name occurrence.

Source: https://www.v-kleist.com/FG/Muttrin/Muttrin.pdf, printed pp. 5–6, lines 293–320 in the accessible text.

The 2021/2022 *Urkundenbuch*, Urkunde 125, dated Belgard 13 April 1477, reproduces the text of Duke Bugislaw X's collective fief and common-hand letter. The edition says it is based on a 16th-century authenticated copy in the Landesarchiv (Tit. IX. Sect. 88. Nr. I. fol. 1), with further transsumpts/retranssumpts listed in note 128.

The charter names:
- Bisprowe at Muttrin with sons Hennige, Petern and Hanse;
- Dubbeszlaff at Damen with sons Drewese, Curde, Bisprowe, Petern and Pribbetzlaue;
- Pribbeslaff at Muttrin with sons Reymer and Bartes;
- brothers at Tychow and Dubberow, and the Vilnow and Raddatz representatives.

The charter itself says the Kleists present testified that they descended from one “Older Vader” who had three sons, from whom came the Muttrin-Damen, Tychow-Dubberow, and Vilnow-Raddatz branches. The edition's explanatory footnotes assign genealogical numbers to some people, but those are editorial identifications and must be kept distinct from the medieval wording itself.

This is valuable evidence for the existence and structure of the three named branches by 1477. It does not independently prove every earlier generation back to 1364, and it does not by itself connect the 1477 men to Cartz Ulrik or the Danish Gedde branch.

## Limitations and next steps

1. **The von Kleist ancestors themselves (Hans Clest through Christian Casimir) have no surviving portraits.** This is normal for minor Pommern nobility before 1700 — their world is documented through feudal letters, not painted portraits. The VKA Dubberow history contains some family-tree diagrams but not individual portraits for these men.

2. **The Corfitz Ulfeldt portrait on Picryl** (https://picryl.com/topics/corfitz+ulfeldt) is of the famous traitor Corfitz Ulfeldt (1606-1664), NOT of our ancestor Knud Ulfeldt (1609-1657). They were cousins. Do not mix up.

3. **Evald Gaardsted-Jørgensen (1922-2008)** has a Litteratursiden profile but no actual photograph in the public sources — his books are listed but no author photo. If you have a family photo, the best route is to scan and add it to the `images/` folder in the sloev/tree repo.

4. **Liss Gaardsted-Jørgensen (1931-2002)** — no public photo located. She was a private person and a kordegn; no obituary with photo has been found in the public online sources.

5. **The Facebook group post** (https://www.facebook.com/groups/7986147284/posts/10163033146982285) about Evald's novel "Skriv hjem, Niels" — may have a cover photo with his portrait. Worth checking if you have Facebook access.

6. **The MyHeritage BillionGraves entry for Lorenz Frederiksen** (https://www.myheritage.dk/research/collection-10147/billiongraves?itemId=1483464614) has the grave photo at Sankt Jørgens Kirkegård, Svendborg. A MyHeritage account may be needed to view it.

7. **Hans Clest of Tychow (c.1400)** — the village of Tychowo (now in Poland) has a parish church and possibly old records, but no portrait of Hans himself has survived.

8. **To extend image coverage**, consider:
   - Visiting Frederiksborgmuseet (where many Rosenkrantz portraits are held) to request scans
   - Searching the Danish National Museum's portrait database (https://www.natmus.dk/)
   - Checking Rigsarkivet's probate records for any portrait inventories of deceased estates
